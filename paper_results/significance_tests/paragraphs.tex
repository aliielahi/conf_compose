\paragraph{Statistical Significance.}
We test whether a composition rule consistently improves on a baseline with exact one-sided paired sign tests \citep{dixon1946sign}. Each dataset and model group forms one paired observation, giving 75 blocks per protocol (15 groups $\times$ 5 datasets); within a block, both systems are scored on the same held-out questions and the same selected answers. The sign test uses only the direction of each difference, which suits our setting because a gain of a given size does not carry the same meaning on every benchmark. For each protocol (before interaction and after each debate round), we compare log-odds Learned Weights with the best single agent, CAGE-CAL and the LLM-elicited judge, and control the family-wise error rate over these nine comparisons with Holm's procedure \citep{holm1979}, separately for AUARC (primary) and p-Brier (secondary). Two secondary analyses use the same blocks: a two-sided comparison of log-odds and probability-space Learned Weights under each confidence estimator, and a test of whether the gain over the best agent grows from one stage of interaction to the next. Because groups share models and the groups of a dataset share questions, the blocks are not fully independent; we therefore read the tests as evidence of consistent improvement rather than as exact error rates. Full results are in Appendix~\ref{app:significance}.

Table~\ref{tab:significance} reports the primary comparisons. Log-odds Learned Weights improves on all three baselines in every protocol and on both metrics: it wins 57 to 75 of the 75 blocks on AUARC and 66 to 74 on p-Brier, and every comparison remains significant after Holm correction ($p_{\mathrm{Holm}}<.0001$).

Table~\ref{tab:significance_interaction} tests whether interaction enlarges these gains. The AUARC gain over the group's best agent is larger after the first debate round than before interaction in 73 of the 75 blocks, and larger after the second round than after the first in 69 ($p_{\mathrm{Holm}}<.0001$ for both). The p-Brier gain grows from the first to the second round (62 of 75 blocks, $p_{\mathrm{Holm}}<.0001$) but not from voting to the first round (40 of 75, $p_{\mathrm{Holm}}=.32$).

Table~\ref{tab:significance_spaces} compares the two pooling spaces with two-sided tests. With sequence probability, log-odds pooling is better on both metrics in every protocol (60 to 70 of the 75 blocks, $p_{\mathrm{Holm}}<.0001$). With consistency, whose confidences take only six values, the two spaces almost coincide: log-odds pooling ranks better (52 to 56 of the 75 blocks on AUARC), probability space is marginally better calibrated (log-odds pooling wins only 24 to 26 blocks on p-Brier), and the median differences stay below 0.07 points on both metrics. Summing log-odds pays off when confidences are fine-grained enough to express near-certainty.

\paragraph{Friedman's Rank Test.}
To summarize all systems at once, we also apply Friedman's test followed by Nemenyi critical differences \citep{friedman1937ranks,nemenyi1963distribution,demsar2006statistical}. Within each of the 75 blocks, we rank the systems of Table~\ref{tab:results_main} on one metric, with rank 1 for the best system. Friedman's test asks whether the average ranks differ more than chance would allow, and the Nemenyi critical difference (CD) is the smallest gap between two average ranks that is significant at $\alpha=.05$. Table~\ref{tab:rankings} shows that the ranks differ in every protocol and on both metrics ($p<.0001$). Log-odds Learned Weights has the best average AUARC rank in all three protocols. On p-Brier, probability-space Learned Weights ranks first, but log-odds Learned Weights stays within the critical difference of it, and every composition rule ranks ahead of the best agent, CAGE-CAL and the LLM-elicited judge.
